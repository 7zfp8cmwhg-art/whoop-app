## Neural control of muscle (mechanism)
- Three-node control system: upper motor neurons (motor cortex, deliberate movement) -> spinal cord -> lower motor neurons (release acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction to trigger contraction).
- Central pattern generators (CPGs) in the spinal cord drive rhythmic/reflexive movement (e.g., walking) without deliberate top-down control; deliberate movement engages upper motor neuron control over this circuit.
- Henneman size principle: motor units are recruited in a fixed low-to-high threshold staircase order (energy-conservation principle) — this happens regardless of light or heavy load, provided effort/duration is sufficient to keep recruiting units.
- Common misconception debunked: reaching high-threshold motor units does NOT require heavy weight specifically — sustained effort across a wide load range (~30-80% of 1RM) can recruit them.

## Resistance training intensity & progression
- Three stimuli that drive muscle adaptation (strength/size/quality): stress, tension, and damage — not all three need to be present simultaneously, but some stress must exist.
- Strength goal = moving progressively greater loads using muscles as an integrated system; hypertrophy goal = isolating a specific muscle and generating hard, near-painful localized contractions.
- Effective load range for both hypertrophy and strength: 30-80% of one-rep max (1RM); heavy weight is not required, though powerlifters aiming for max strength do need to train near-heavy.
- Weekly set volume (per muscle group): ~5 sets/week is the minimum to maintain existing muscle; 5-15 sets/week is the most broadly beneficial range for improving strength/hypertrophy; range can extend to 20, even 25-30 sets/week for trained individuals, though very efficient responders may find high volume counterproductive.
- Recommended failure ratio: only ~10% of total sets/workouts should be trained to true muscular failure; the majority of volume should stop short of failure to permit greater total training volume.
- Explosiveness/speed training protocol: move loads at 60-75% of 1RM as quickly as possible in controlled fashion, never to failure (form/speed degrades near failure).
- Rest between sets: ~2 minutes specifically for the "testosterone protocol" (attributed to Duncan French and colleagues); 2-6 minutes rest is beneficial for hypertrophy and strength gains generally.
- Underlying research attributed to Andy Galpin, Brad Schoenfeld, and Mike Roberts (exercise physiologists).

## Recovery assessment (systemic nervous system recovery)
- Three ways to test recovery from any training modality (resistance, running, swimming): HRV, grip strength, CO2 tolerance test.
- HRV (heart rate variability): heart rate slows on exhale / speeds on inhale via diaphragm-heart-brain connection; useful but hard for most people to measure/track.
- Grip strength test: measure first thing each morning (via hand dynamometer or squeezing a scale) against a well-rested baseline; a 10-20%+ reduction signals the nerve-to-muscle pathways are still recovering (not necessarily muscle fatigue) — track trend over time, not absolute performance.
- CO2 tolerance / breath-holding discard-rate test protocol: (1) inhale through nose fully then exhale fully, repeat this cycle 4 times; (2) on the 5th breath, inhale maximally through the nose with diaphragmatic (belly-out) breathing; (3) start a timer and exhale as slowly as possible through the mouth (like through a tiny straw) until you can no longer expel any air — that duration is the "CO2 discard/blow-off time."
  - <20-25 seconds: not recovered from prior day's training.
  - ~30-60 seconds: "green zone" — ready for more physical work.
  - 65-120 seconds: nervous system considered fully recovered (not necessarily individual muscles).
  - Typical ~30 sec; 20 sec = fast; 2 minutes = "heroic."

## Recovery interventions — what helps vs. hinders
- Cold/ice baths: reduce inflammation and delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS), but appear to blunt mTOR pathway signaling and other inflammation-linked pathways needed for muscle repair/growth — using ice baths immediately after resistance training may short-circuit strength/hypertrophy adaptations.
- NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs/common painkillers): shown to blunt gains in endurance, strength, and muscle size from exercise — advises caution especially within 4 hours before and 4 hours after training.

## Nutrition for muscle
- Sodium (salt) is essential for neuron firing (nerve-to-muscle electrical signaling); insufficient salt impairs nerve/brain/muscle communication. Salt needs vary with water intake, caffeine intake, food intake, diuretic use, heat, and sweat rate. Also need adequate potassium and magnesium.
- Creatine: ~5 g/day recommended for a ~180 lb person. Cites 66 studies showing creatine increases power output by 12-20% across sprinting, running, jumping, and weightlifting; also improves cellular hydration (draws water into cells) and reduces fatigue.
- Leucine (essential amino acid): recommends ingesting 700-3,000 mg of leucine per meal to support muscle protein synthesis, preferably from whole foods rather than supplements alone.
- Protein "density": animal proteins (steak, chicken, fish, eggs) provide a higher density of essential amino acids per calorie than equivalent-calorie plant sources (beans, nuts), though plant sources can still supply all essential amino acids and support muscle growth.
- Practical eating pattern: eating 2-4 times per day while ensuring sufficient essential amino acid (especially leucine) intake is sufficient to support muscle repair, growth, and strength gains, regardless of dietary ethics/pattern (omnivore, vegan, vegetarian).
