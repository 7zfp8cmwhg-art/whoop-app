## Three signals that drive food preference/choice
- Signal 1: taste on the tongue/mouth — five basic tastes (bitter, sweet, umami, salty, sour) detected by chemical receptors, transduced to electrical signals via the gustatory nerve, synapsing in the brainstem nucleus of the solitary tract, then projecting to the insular cortex (the brain's interoception hub).
- Signal 2: subconscious gut signaling — specialized gut sensory cells called "neuropod cells" detect amino acids, sugars, and fatty acids in the digestive tract and send signals via the nodose ganglion to the brain, triggering dopamine release (motivation/reward/seeking) independent of conscious taste perception.
- Signal 3: belief/learned association — subjective belief about what a food contains/does can directly alter physiological measures (blood glucose, insulin release), not just subjective enjoyment; described as a genuine "belief effect," distinct from placebo.
- Taste/food-preference circuitry is described as "soft-wired" (some innate/hardwired preference for sweetness, but substantially modifiable through learned association), not fixed.

## Key experimental findings on sweetness/dopamine/blood sugar
- Sweet substance that also raises blood glucose: dopamine rises (expected).
- Non-caloric artificial sweetener alone: initially not much preferred and doesn't raise dopamine (no blood glucose change) — but with repeated ingestion over time, dopamine responses to the artificial sweetener eventually rise anyway (a learned/conditioned response).
- Pairing an artificial sweetener with a separate food that raises blood glucose (e.g., diet soda alongside high-glycemic food) creates Pavlovian conditioning: later, consuming the artificial sweetener ALONE triggers increased insulin secretion, even though it has no calories itself — i.e., it can disrupt blood sugar/insulin regulation via learned association.
- Practical extraction: if consuming artificial sweeteners/diet soda, best to consume them separately from (not alongside) foods that raise blood glucose, to avoid conditioning an insulin response to the sweetener itself.
- Cites work by Stanford psychologist Alia Crum: subjects given an identical milkshake but told it was either "low-calorie/healthy" or "high-calorie/indulgent" showed different physiological responses (blood glucose and insulin changes) matching the belief, not the actual shake content — a genuine belief effect on physiology, not mere subjective preference.
- Repeatedly pairing a food you want to like with something that shifts brain metabolism (raises blood glucose/dopamine) can make that food taste subjectively better within about 7 days, reliably within 14 days — a practical method for acquiring a taste for healthy-but-unpalatable foods.
- Cited review: Ivan de Araujo, Mark Schatzker (referred to as "Shaker" in transcript), and Dana Small — "Rethinking Food Reward," Annual Review of Psychology, 2019 — on food reward vs. food reinforcement as distinct phenomena.

## Brain-supportive nutrients/foods (with target intakes)
- Essential fatty acids / phospholipids form the structural fat of neuronal membranes (distinct from storage/adipose fat) and are the top brain-supportive food category.
- Omega-3s (EPA/DHA): most people get enough omega-6 but not enough omega-3. Sources: fish, chia seeds, walnuts, soybeans, other plant foods.
- Recommended omega-3/EPA intake: above 1.5 g/day, ideally 2-3 g/day of EPA for cognitive benefit (short and long term).
- Phosphatidylserine: abundant in meat and fish; supports neuronal function directly; relatively inexpensive to supplement if not eating enough meat/fish.
- Choline: precursor for acetylcholine (a neuromodulator central to focus/attention, not a classic neurotransmitter); many Alzheimer's drugs target the acetylcholine pathway. Best dietary source: eggs (especially yolks). Recommended target: ~500 mg to 1 g (1,000 mg) choline per day. Non-egg sources (lower amounts): potatoes, nuts, seeds, grains, fruit.
- Creatine: derived from meat or supplemented; can be used as a brain fuel source; some evidence it enhances frontal cortical circuit function connected to mood regulation/motivation. Threshold dose for cognitive benefit: at least 5 g/day of creatine monohydrate — shown to improve cognition even in people not getting creatine from animal sources. Huberman personally takes 5 g/day long-term as a "baseline insurance policy," alongside EPAs and alpha-GPC.
- Anthocyanins (found in blueberries, blackberries, dark currants, other thin-skinned purplish berries): supported by data for improved brain function, mechanism unclear (direct neuronal effect vs. anti-inflammatory vs. other). Suggested intake: roughly a cup or two of berries fairly often/daily.
- Glutamine: some (described as "somewhat scant") evidence for immune system support and for reducing sugar cravings via glutamine-sensing gut neurons that signal satiation/satisfaction to the brain. Food sources: cottage cheese, beef, chicken, fish, dairy, eggs; plant sources include beans, cabbage, spinach, parsley. Supplemental dose range cited: 1 g up to 10 g/day.
- All the above nutrients are obtainable from food; supplementation is presented as a way to reach higher target levels conveniently (e.g., if you don't eat much fish), not a strict requirement.
