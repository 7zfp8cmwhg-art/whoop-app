# Eating for Better Sleep & Foods that Improve Metabolic Health | Dr. Marie-Pierre St-Onge
- Guest: Dr. Marie-Pierre St-Onge, professor of nutritional medicine, Institute of Human Nutrition, Columbia University
- URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jjaFnKtytqI

## Sleep loss -> increased hunger/intake (sex-specific mechanisms)
- Controlled crossover study: participants given 9-hr time-in-bed (slept ~7.5h) vs 4-hr time-in-bed (slept ~3h50m) for 5 consecutive nights, first 3 days on controlled isocaloric feeding, last day self-selected food intake.
- In men, short sleep increased ghrelin (the hormone that triggers food intake). In women, short sleep instead reduced GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1, a satiety hormone) — a sex-specific split that was an incidental, unplanned finding (prior all-male studies had only shown the ghrelin effect).
- On the self-select day, participants ate ~300 more calories in the short-sleep condition than after adequate sleep.
- Neuroimaging showed upregulation of brain reward centers in response to food stimuli under sleep restriction vs. adequate sleep.
- Meta-analyses across studies show sleep restriction causes roughly 250-400 calories/day of overeating on average.
- Naima Covassin (2022 paper): sleep restriction of ~5 hrs/night vs 7.5 hrs/night for 2 weeks led to ~0.5 kg (half a kilo) weight gain in that 2-week period with no other behavior change.

## Sleep restriction and metabolic/cardiovascular markers
- In the acute 5-night severe restriction study (isocaloric, controlled feeding), cortisol, glucose, and insulin curves were statistically identical between adequate and short sleep conditions ("superimposable") — no measurable hormonal/metabolic derangement from sleep loss alone when food intake and environment are tightly controlled.
- Energy expenditure while awake was actually slightly higher (~5%, ~90 kcal) under sleep restriction (measured in a metabolic chamber) since staying awake is more energetically costly than sleeping — but this was far smaller than the ~300 kcal excess intake, netting a positive energy balance.
- Follow-up 6-week mild chronic sleep restriction study: good sleepers (baseline ~7.5h) reduced sleep by 1.5h/night to ~6h/night (a realistic "insufficient sleep" duration) for 6 weeks in free-living conditions. Result: increased insulin resistance, reduced insulin sensitivity (effect worse in postmenopausal vs. premenopausal women), and increased blood pressure.
- Interpretation: population-level cardiometabolic harms of short sleep (higher CVD risk, hypertension, type 2 diabetes risk) likely reflect a combination of sleep loss plus resulting poorer food choices/increased sedentary behavior in free-living conditions, since the short, tightly-controlled experiment alone didn't move glucose/insulin/lipids.

## Diet's effect on sleep architecture (bidirectional relationship)
- In-lab study: when participants were allowed to self-select food (vs. researcher-controlled diet), they ate ~450 more calories/day, ~33% more saturated fat, slightly less protein, slightly more carbohydrate.
- On the self-selected diet, participants took over 70% longer to fall asleep, and slow-wave (deep) sleep was ~20-23% shorter than on the controlled diet — despite going to bed at the same time (10pm) both nights.
- Same-day nutrient associations with that night's polysomnography-measured sleep: higher fiber intake -> more deep sleep; higher saturated fat intake -> less deep sleep; more refined carbohydrates/simple sugars -> more "arousals" (shifts from deeper to lighter sleep stages, not necessarily full awakenings — meaning less deep/REM sleep even without waking up).
- St-Onge's personal practice: eats last meal at least 3 hours before bed; notes eating close to bedtime raises body temperature (thermic effect of food) which can interfere with the cooling needed for sleep onset.
- Population studies (Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis, MESA, with Susan Redline): a diet more closely aligned with the Mediterranean pattern was associated with better probability of adequate sleep duration and fewer insomnia symptoms.
- Women's Health Initiative longitudinal analysis (published earlier in the year of this episode): women whose diets aligned with Mediterranean or DASH patterns were less likely to develop or sustain insomnia over a 3-year follow-up.
- DASH diet (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension): high fruit/vegetable/nut/seed intake, low-fat dairy, more plant-based, tested in both low-salt and regular-salt versions. Some individuals are salt-sensitive (reduced salt significantly lowers their blood pressure) while others are not, but DASH outperformed a typical American-style diet regardless of its salt content.

## Circadian timing of meals / metabolism
- Controlled feeding study varying eating-window start time (1 hr after waking vs. 5 hrs after waking, both with same 10-hr eating window, identical food/quantity/timing, measured in a metabolic chamber): meals eaten later in the day produced less fat oxidation than identical meals eaten earlier.
- Practical takeaway given: shift the majority of daily caloric intake to roughly the first two-thirds of the waking day; avoid large or late dinners.
- Marta Garaulet (Spain) weight-loss study: participants who ate their largest meal (lunch) earlier in the day had significantly better weight-loss outcomes than those who ate it later — even within a culture (Spain) that eats comparatively late overall.

## Sleep duration, aging, and sex differences
- Cites a colleague's newly-published paper (Nature) on biological clocks and organ-specific aging: the "sweet spot" sleep duration for optimal aging across most organs was ~6.5 to 7.5-8 hours, with a U-shaped curve (both too little and too much sleep associated with worse aging markers). Optimal duration was slightly longer for women; the U-shape was more pronounced in men for some organs.
- Women sleep somewhat longer than men on average across the lifespan, yet report worse subjective sleep quality and more insomnia symptoms (difficulty falling/staying asleep) than men.
- Women are more sensitive than men to the metabolic consequences of poor sleep — e.g., blood pressure rises at lower sleep-apnea severity thresholds in women compared to men (from a review paper on hypertension, sleep, and sex differences).
- American Heart Association scientific statement (co-authored) on "multidimensional sleep health": recommends clinicians ask open-ended questions like "how's your sleep?" rather than only asking about duration, since sleep health includes regularity, quality, satisfaction, and daytime alertness/vigilance, not just hours slept.

## Sleep apnea
- Sleep apnea is throat-based obstruction (airway/trachea closure), distinct from simple snoring; nasal strips can reduce snoring but do not address the underlying apnea.
- Weight loss is typically the first-line intervention when excess weight is present; CPAP is standard treatment but requires professionally determined pressure settings via a sleep study — cannot simply buy and self-configure one.
- Diagnosis options: in-lab polysomnography (gold standard) or in-home sleep testing, both requiring a doctor's referral.

## Napping
- No direct personal research on napping, but general guidance cited: keep naps short (30 min, no more than 1 hour) and early in the day, to avoid dissipating sleep pressure too close to bedtime (which creates a vicious cycle of poor nighttime sleep).
- Chronic need for naps despite adequate nighttime sleep opportunity suggests an underlying nighttime sleep-quality problem that should be investigated rather than compensated for with naps.

## Specific foods/nutrients studied (functional foods research)
- Kefir (St-Onge's Master's thesis at McGill): men with mildly elevated cholesterol given 2 cups (~500 mL)/day of kefir vs. regular milk for 1 month — null result, no effect on cholesterol synthesis.
- Mannan-oligosaccharide-enriched coffee (industry-sponsored replication study): extracted from spent coffee grounds (not present in normally brewed coffee), tested vs. placebo beverage; produced statistically significant greater fat loss in men but not in women — product was not brought to market due to the sex-specific effect.
- Ginger: dissolved ginger powder in warm water as a single dose; measured thermic effect of food (energy expenditure via metabolic hood) over 4-5 hours — found a significant increase in energy expenditure, hypothesized to work through capsaicin/TRP-type receptor activation.
- Medium-chain triglycerides (MCT oil, St-Onge's PhD work): MCTs (C8/C10 fatty acids) are transported directly to the liver and metabolized/burned more readily than long-chain triglycerides (C12+), which travel through peripheral circulation and are more readily stored as adipose tissue.
  - MCT meals increased thermic effect of food by ~45-60 kcal per meal versus a saturated-fat-matched control (beef tallow) — small per-meal effect but statistically significant, found in both men and women across two studies.
  - Follow-up weight-loss trial: MCT oil produced greater weight loss than olive oil over the study period.
  - Practical dose suggested: roughly 1-2 tablespoons per day, used in place of (not in addition to) another cooking oil.
- Fermented foods vs. fiber (Stanford's Sonnenburg lab, referenced): a crossover study comparing increased fiber intake vs. increased low-sugar fermented food intake on inflammatory markers ("inflammatome") found fermented foods consistently reduced inflammation, while increased fiber had mixed effects — many participants' inflammation markers rose with more fiber, suggesting fiber type/source matters and needs better characterization; fermented food intake was ramped up to ~4 servings/day.
- Portfolio diet (David Jenkins): combination originally of soy protein, nuts, plant sterols, and soluble fiber (now expanded to include legumes and olive oil/monounsaturated fats) achieved cholesterol reduction comparable to a statin drug in a head-to-head trial.
- Frito-Lay-funded 25-day, 3-arm crossover snack study: corn-oil-fried chips (Doritos/Fritos/Cheetos/Tostitos-style) produced a better lipid profile and lower lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] than either a low-fat/high-carb snack or a high-fat/high-saturated-fat snack alternative.
- Discussion of protein per meal: cites Alan Aragon that the old claim of a ~30g-per-meal protein absorption ceiling is false — the body can utilize up to ~100g of protein in a sitting (under some conditions, e.g. with exercise); ~95% of the muscle-protein-synthesis benefit of adequate protein intake can be achieved with roughly 2 meals plus a small snack, evenly or unevenly distributed.

## Practical/behavioral notes
- Reinforces a bidirectional "vicious cycle vs. virtuous cycle" framing: poor sleep -> poor food choices -> worse sleep, or good sleep -> good food choices -> better sleep.
- St-Onge personally uses a ~3-hour buffer between last food intake and bedtime, citing both cardiometabolic benefit and personal comfort (avoiding feeling overheated near bedtime).
