# How to Heal From Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) | Dr. Victor Carrión
- Guest: Dr. Victor Carrión (Vice Chair, Psychiatry & Behavioral Sciences, Stanford; director, Stanford Early Life Stress and Resilience Program)
- URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4RFEkGKKhdE

## Stress vs. trauma framework
- Stress operates as an inverted U-shaped curve: moderate stress improves performance/health up to an optimal point, beyond which outcomes decline — the decline phase is where traumatic stress and allostatic load occur.
- Distinction: homeostasis = normal coping range; allostasis = when stress exceeds coping capacity and produces a physiological cost to the body.
- Traumatic stress specifically involves a perceived threat to physical integrity, not just a demand to cope.
- "PTSD feeds on avoidance" — pretending an event didn't happen, delaying treatment, and avoidance behaviors (including substance abuse, self-injury) are what entrench and worsen PTSD over time.
- Carrión prefers the term "post-traumatic stress injury" over "disorder" — frames it as a desensitized/dysregulated autonomic nervous system requiring treatment/recalibration, akin to a physical injury, rather than an inherent psychiatric flaw.
- PTSD in kids often arises from cumulative/repeated stressors ("a backpack" of accumulated stress) rather than a single traumatic event — example given: children in Haiti after an earthquake were more affected by ongoing poverty/violence than the earthquake itself.
- Cited study: Vietnam veterans with a history of childhood maltreatment had significantly higher PTSD prevalence after combat than veterans without that history — supporting the cumulative-stress model.

## Children vs. adults
- Epidemiological data confirms children are more vulnerable to PTSD than adults, likely due to greater neuroplasticity — but that same plasticity means children also have greater potential for recovery/resilience with the right support.
- Carrión disputes the phrase "children are resilient" — he argues children are inherently more vulnerable but can become resilient given support and tools.
- PTSD symptoms in children without meeting full DSM-5 diagnostic criteria are termed "pediatric PTSS" (post-traumatic stress symptoms) — high comorbidity with anxiety and depression.

## Cortisol findings (Carrión's research)
- In children with PTSD symptoms, the normal circadian cortisol rhythm (high on waking, declining through the day, small bumps after meals/stress) was intact, EXCEPT pre-bedtime cortisol remained abnormally elevated instead of dropping to low levels like healthy controls.
- This elevated evening/nighttime cortisol correlated clinically with the kids' nighttime symptoms: enuresis (bed-wetting), nightmares, insufficient/shallow sleep, nighttime fears.
- MRI structural studies: longitudinal data showed a correlation between higher pre-bedtime cortisol and smaller hippocampal volume in youth with PTSD symptoms (no difference found cross-sectionally in the first study).
- Functional MRI: healthy kids activated significantly more hippocampal "voxels" during memory tasks than kids with PTSD symptoms; similar reduced activation seen in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex during executive-function and emotional-face tasks.
- Interpretation: a "malfunctioning frontostriatal/frontolimbic pathway" — an amygdala that activates quickly/strongly to emotional stimuli (hypervigilance) paired with a weakened prefrontal "brake" on that amygdala reactivity.
- Referenced background context: work by Robert Sapolsky and Bruce McEwen on glucocorticoid neurotoxicity in limbic/cortical brain regions (areas with high glucocorticoid receptor density) informed why cortisol dysregulation was investigated as a mechanism.
- Notes an association between chronic severe mental illness (in general, not just PTSD) and dying approximately 25 years younger than the general population — attributed in part to accelerated aging from chronic stress.

## Distinguishing PTSD from ADHD
- Hypervigilance is often misdiagnosed as hyperactivity, and dissociation ("checking out") is often misdiagnosed as inattentiveness — leading to inappropriate ADHD diagnoses in traumatized children.
- Clinical differentiator: true ADHD hyperactivity is persistent/steady across environments and people; PTSD-driven hyperarousal is intermittent ("a jagged line"), triggered on-and-off specifically by trauma-related cues.
- Similarly, ADHD inattention is inconsistent (kids with ADHD can hyperfocus on highly engaging stimuli like video games) whereas PTSD-related dissociation is cue-triggered.
- Misdiagnosing PTSD as ADHD and treating with stimulant medication does not address the underlying condition and can worsen hyperarousability; correct first-line treatment for PTSD (acute or chronic) is psychosocial intervention, not medication.
- Both conditions can coexist; ADHD itself can also increase vulnerability to developing PTSD (reduced situational attentiveness).

## Trauma cues / triggers
- Trauma "cues" (triggers) are typically neutral sensory stimuli associated with the traumatic event via classical conditioning (e.g., a color, a rain, a particular vocal timbre/pitch) — not the threat itself (e.g., a gun is a threat, not a "cue").
- Identifying one's specific cues is described as clinically valuable: it normalizes symptom onset (reduces feeling "crazy" or "bad") and enables targeted intervention, even though not all cues can ever be fully identified.
- Example given: hypervigilance learned from one dangerous context (e.g., running to a room during domestic violence) can generalize maladaptively to unrelated settings (e.g., fleeing a noisy classroom), producing consequences when the new context is not understood by observers (teachers, principals).

## Q-Centered Therapy (Carrión's treatment developed at Stanford)
- "Q" = cue-centered therapy, developed for youth with PTSD/PTSS; published as a therapist manual by Oxford University Press ("Cue-Centered Therapy for Youth Experiencing Post-Traumatic Symptoms").
- Multimodal/hybrid approach — combines CBT with self-efficacy, empowerment, and insight-oriented work; designed so parental participation is not mandatory (important because parents are sometimes the source of trauma or unavailable due to work/practical constraints).
- Shown in trials to reduce anxiety, depression, and PTSD symptom scores as rated by both the child and independent observers/parents; even non-participating parents' own anxiety decreased when their child's symptoms improved.
- Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) used (cheaper/more portable than fMRI, good temporal resolution, but only images cortical/outer brain regions, unlike fMRI's access to deep limbic structures) — cortical prefrontal activity patterns predicted treatment response specifically for kids receiving Q-Centered Therapy or trauma-focused CBT (compared to treatment-as-usual).
- "Toolbox" concept: each child/patient builds a personalized, self-generated set of coping tools (breathing exercises, muscle relaxation, positive-thought practice, mindfulness, simple yoga poses like mountain pose, talking to friends/teammates, listening to music, etc.) — tools the child develops themselves reportedly work better than those taught directly by the therapist.
- "Four corners" (four-quadrant) assessment framework for examining a stress response/behavior: (1) cognitive/thinking, (2) emotional/feeling labels, (3) somatic/physical sensations, (4) actions/behavior — an extension of the standard CBT thought-feeling-behavior triangle, adding the somatic corner because children often lack vocabulary and instead report physical complaints (headache, stomachache).
- Therapeutic principle: working on just one "corner" (e.g., reframing a cognitive distortion) can cascade and change the other three, producing a new adaptive response.
- Emotional-intensity gauge used during sessions: a 0-10 (or similar) "emotional thermometer" (attributed to Wagner) — the four-corners exercise is only used when a child's distress is moderate (not at 9-10), since at peak distress they should use a toolbox coping tool first rather than engage in cognitive work.
- Common cognitive distortions addressed include "it was my fault" reframed to "I'm not a victim, I'm a survivor."

## Prevention program: school-based yoga/mindfulness (with Pure Power Inc.)
- Piloted in East Palo Alto schools (under-resourced district relative to Palo Alto) starting roughly 10 years before the recording; taught by classroom teachers (not outside yoga instructors) 2-3 times per week for 15-50 minutes, done in regular clothes on mats, in the classroom.
- Randomized controlled trial used a demographically comparable but geographically separate San Jose school district as delayed-treatment control (a whole-district-level control was necessary because individual classroom/school-level control groups suffered contamination from social diffusion of the practices).
- Key outcome: the intervention increased average sleep duration by 73 minutes per night in participating students (measured via at-home portable polysomnography, in collaboration with a researcher named Ruth [surname unclear in transcript, "oara"]), and increased sleep depth/REM and deep sleep.
- Preliminary (unpublished at time of interview) data showed reduced amygdala activity pre- vs. post-intervention in participating kids.
- The original pilot's anecdotal signal: over ~3 months, none of the students in the two piloted classrooms were sent to the principal's office for disciplinary issues (versus typical rates), which prompted the larger controlled study.
- Currently being scaled island-wide in Puerto Rico: all teachers trained in the yoga/mindfulness curriculum, all school counselors trained in Q-Centered Therapy, with baseline and follow-up assessment of PTSD/anxiety/depression symptoms plus screening and referral of at-risk students to trained counselors.

## Genomics / organoid research on resilience biology
- Collaboration with Stanford colleagues Alex Urban (genetics) and postdoc Caroline Perman using brain "organoids" (mini 3D neuron clusters grown from stem cells, reprogrammed from skin/fibroblast cells via Yamanaka factors) to model trauma at the cellular level.
- Organoids (confirmed to express active glucocorticoid receptors) were exposed to different cortisol concentrations calibrated (with input from Robert Sapolsky) to mimic trauma-level cortisol exposure, sub-trauma "cue"-level exposure, or no exposure, then compared via epigenetic analysis.
- Found expected PTSD-associated genes (e.g., glucocorticoid receptor genes) altered, plus a novel finding: genes related to collagen formation were also affected — linked by Carrión to known associations between chronic stress, atherosclerosis, and accelerated biological aging.
- Plan: correlate these organoid/epigenetic findings with buccal swab epigenetic data from the Puerto Rico student cohort (comparing kids' stress exposure, intervention status, and treatment response) to identify genes that may be protective against high cortisol/stress — an attempt to characterize the biology of resilience.

## Resilience — conceptual framing
- Carrión reframes "resilience" (bouncing back to baseline) as "adaptation" (bouncing back to a better place than before), using the COVID-19 pandemic as an example.
- Known (non-mechanistic) resilience factors: sense of humor, perseverance, and presence of a supportive adult in a child's life who provides opportunity and someone to talk to.
- Notes current science has very limited understanding of the underlying biology of resilience — motivating the organoid/genomics research above.

## Miscellaneous
- Discussion of deliberate cold exposure (reference to Craig Heller, Stanford) framed primarily as a controllable, safe way to practice voluntary regulation of the sympathetic/adrenaline stress response and build "frontal control over limbic pathways" — not focused on metabolic or inflammation claims.
- Notes David Spiegel's point that a key factor in whether a stressor is harmful is whether the person had control over entering/exiting the stressful state (relevant contrast with trauma, where the person had no choice).
