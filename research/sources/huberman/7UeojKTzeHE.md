# Using Light (Sunlight, Blue Light & Red Light) to Optimize Health | Huberman Lab Essentials

## Basic physics/biology of light
- Light is electromagnetic energy; different wavelengths penetrate tissue to different depths, and biological effects come from absorbance/reflectance/transmission of light through specific cells or organelles.
- Three main routes light affects biology: retinal photoreceptors (rods/cones plus intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, ipRGCs), skin (epidermis: keratinocytes and melanocytes), and direct cellular effects (e.g., mitochondria) in any tissue light can reach; deep organs like the spleen are affected only indirectly (via neural/hormonal relays), not by direct photon exposure.

## Melatonin as a light-driven "calendar" signal
- ipRGCs (melanopsin-containing ganglion cells) detect short-wavelength light and signal brain centers that suppress the pineal gland's melatonin release; melatonin duration each day/night acts as an internal readout of day length across the year (more melatonin release in winter in the northern hemisphere).
- Endogenous melatonin has two broad roles: "regulatory" (e.g., positively affects bone mass; involved in pubertal maturation of gonads, generally suppressive on ovarian/testicular maturation — chronically high melatonin in children helps delay puberty) and "protective" effects; it is also a strong modulator of placental development, so pregnant people should consult physicians before melatonin supplementation.
- Huberman reiterates his standing skepticism of melatonin supplements: typical doses are far above physiological levels ("super physiological").
- Bright light exposure at night (e.g., flipping on bright bathroom lights) can crash nighttime melatonin toward zero; occasional exposure is fine, but chronic nightly disruption undermines a fundamental circadian signal.
- Recommendation: get more outdoor light in spring/summer, allow more indoor time in winter; for seasonal affective disorder, get more bright light (including morning) during fall/winter.

## UVB exposure: hormones, mood, fertility
- Cited study (Cell Reports): "skin exposure to UVB light induces a skin brain gonad axis and sexual behavior" — in mice and humans, sufficient UVB exposure to skin (not eyes) raised testosterone and estrogen (proportionally, in both sexes) within a short time window, increased mate-seeking in mice, and increased gonadal (testes/ovary) size in mice past a threshold dose.
- In female mice, UVB increased ovarian follicle/egg maturation (fertility marker).
- Suggested protocol: 2–3 sun exposures per week, minimum 20–30 minutes each, to as much skin surface as reasonably possible, to support testosterone/estrogen and mood/passion.
- UVB (skin and eyes) also raises pain tolerance, which is longer in "longer day" conditions. Cited studies: "skin exposure to ultraviolet B rapidly activates systemic neuroendocrine and immunosuppressive responses" (single UVB exposure raised corticotropin and beta-endorphin) and a Neuron paper on a visual circuit through the periaqueductal gray mediating anti-nociceptive (pain-reducing) effects of bright light via melanopsin-cell input.
- Practical notes: seek UVB primarily from sunlight; cloud cover still passes far more light energy than typical indoor artificial light; never look directly at any light source bright enough to be painful; eyeglasses/contacts are fine (they focus light), but sunglasses, car windshields, and most windows filter out UVB; wearing blue-blockers during the day (rather than just evening) may be counterproductive since UVB/blue light exposure in daytime is desired.
- Amount of skin surface exposed (clothing choice) affects total photon capture and thus hormonal/pain signaling magnitude.
- Recommendation to consider getting UVB exposure year-round (not just summer), based on discussion with the director of the NIMH chronobiology unit ("Samaritar" — likely a mis-transcription), provided no burning/eye damage; those with seasonal depression may benefit from a SAD lamp or a cheaper LED light panel used during the day.
- Caution: people with retinitis pigmentosa, macular degeneration, glaucoma, or high skin-cancer risk should consult an ophthalmologist/dermatologist before increasing UVB exposure. Blind individuals who retain eyes often retain melanopsin ganglion cells and can still benefit from UVB via the eyes.

## UVB, immune function, and tissue turnover
- UVB on the eyes activates sympathetic nervous system pathways that in turn activate the spleen's deployment of immune cells/molecules that fight infection — proposed mechanism for why infections feel less prevalent in spring/summer (immune readiness is higher, not necessarily fewer pathogens); implies winter is when deliberately seeking UVB to support spleen/immune function may matter most.
- UVB exposure (to skin and to eyes) is associated with faster wound healing, faster hair growth, and faster nail growth via triggering turnover of stem cells (in skin, hair follicle niches, and nails); a cited PNAS study showed melanopsin ganglion cell activation in the eye is critical for this stem-cell turnover in skin/hair/nails.

## Nighttime light and mood/dopamine (perihabenular pathway)
- A neural circuit from melanopsin ganglion cells bypasses classic circadian-clock regions and connects to the perihabenular nucleus; activation of this pathway at the wrong time of day (specifically UVB/bright light at night) reduces dopamine output and can worsen mood/contribute to depression.
- Concrete protocol: avoid UVB light exposure from artificial sources between 10 p.m. and 4 a.m.; keep any necessary evening lights dim and positioned low (melanopsin cells are in the lower retina and preferentially sense light from above), to minimize activation of this pathway.

## Red light / near-infrared light: mechanism and skin effects
- Long-wavelength light (red, near-infrared) penetrates deeper into skin (epidermis into dermis) than short-wavelength light, reaching sebaceous glands, melanocytes, hair-follicle stem cells, and — at the cellular level — mitochondria.
- Proposed mechanism: aging/metabolically active cells accumulate reactive oxygen species (ROS), which impairs ATP production; red/NIR light activates mitochondria, raising ATP and reducing ROS, supporting wound healing, scar/pigmentation reduction, and acne-related tissue turnover (described as grounded in real mitochondrial biology, not "woo science").

## Red light for eye/brain aging (Glenn Jeffery lab, UCL)
- Two human studies (~12 weeks in one, a few weeks in the other) had subjects (20s vs. 40–72 years old) view ~670 nm red light from about one foot away for 2–3 minutes/day.
- Result: significant improvement in visual function (visual acuity via the Tritan test, targeting short-wavelength cone function) only in the 40+ age group — a 22% improvement in visual acuity reported.
- Mechanism: rods/cones are among the body's most metabolically active cells and accumulate high ROS with age; red light reduced ROS and partially reversed age-related decline in short/medium-wavelength cone function.
- Also reduced retinal drusen (fatty/cholesterol deposits that accumulate with age in the neural retina).
- Protocol specifics from these studies: light must be viewed early in the day, within the first 3 hours of waking, at 670 nm (red) and complementary 790 nm (near-infrared); light source must not be painful to look at (a "too bright" light causing squinting is a hard stop, since retinal neurons don't regenerate); most commercial red-light panels are too bright for direct close-up eye viewing and are designed for skin illumination (often shipped with eye protection that blocks the red light).

## Red light at night for shift workers
- Cited study: "red light, a novel non-pharmacological intervention to promote alertness in shift workers."
- Dim red light at night does not suppress melatonin and does not raise cortisol (unlike bright/short-wavelength light), making it the preferred light source for people who must be awake late at night (shift workers, parents up with infants, etc.).
- Elevated cortisol late at night (9–10 p.m.) is associated with depression and other mental health issues; general recommendation is cortisol should be high early in the day, not at night.
- Guidance: keep red light as dim as possible while still allowing needed tasks to be performed.
