## Circadian rhythm fundamentals
- The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), a cluster of neurons above the roof of the mouth/behind the eyes, generates an endogenous ~24-hour rhythm and is entrained (matched) to the external light-dark cycle.
- Nearly every physiological system is tethered to this clock: wakefulness, body temperature, metabolism, immune function, and mood.
- Body temperature is the "effector" signal the master clock uses to synchronize peripheral cells/organs (e.g., pancreas cells vs. neurons) to one unified schedule — each tissue interprets the temperature signal to stay in phase.
- Sleepiness correlates with falling body temperature; wakefulness correlates with rising body temperature.

## Light exposure targets and timing
- Rule-of-thumb target: get exposure to at least 100,000 lux before 9-10 AM (ideally before 9 AM), assuming waking between 5-8 AM.
- Outdoor light, even on a cloudy day, can be 7,000-10,000 lux — far brighter than typical indoor lighting.
- The eye-to-clock system sums/integrates photon exposure over time (it's a slow-summing system), so exposure doesn't need to be all at once.
- If natural sunlight is insufficient (e.g., northern latitudes in winter), artificial bright light can substitute, though sunlight has additional beneficial qualities.
- It takes very little light to shift/reset the clock after ~8 PM — much less than what's needed in the morning — so avoid bright (and even moderately bright) light between roughly 10-11 PM and 4 AM.
- Viewing sunlight near sunset is recommended to down-regulate eye sensitivity ahead of night.

## Temperature minimum: the key mechanism
- Your "temperature minimum" is the point in the 24-hour cycle when core body temperature is lowest; it typically falls 90 minutes to 2 hours before average wake time.
- It's a reference point/landmark, not an actual temperature reading — no thermometer needed.
- Light exposure in the 4 hours AFTER temperature minimum → phase ADVANCE (clock shifts earlier; you get up and fall asleep earlier on subsequent days).
- Light exposure in the 4-6 hours BEFORE temperature minimum → phase DELAY (clock shifts later; you wake and sleep later).
- Combining light, exercise, and eating in the appropriate window (not necessarily all three) can shift the clock by roughly 1-3 hours per day.
- Example given: Huberman's own temperature minimum is ~4:30-5:00 AM given his typical 6-7 AM wake time.

## Jet lag mechanics
- Jet lag has two separate components: (1) travel fatigue (non-time-zone-related, e.g., N-S travel) and (2) time-zone jet lag (misalignment between local light/dark and internal endogenous rhythm).
- Traveling eastward is harder than westward because the autonomic nervous system is better at staying alert/awake (pushing the clock later is easy) than at forcing early sleep (advancing the clock is hard).
- Peer-reviewed research indicates jet lag can shorten lifespan, and eastward travel takes a greater toll on longevity than westward travel.
- Jet lag/circadian misalignment worsens with age: melatonin release is high and stable/flat in children, becomes cyclic (once every 24h) at puberty, and becomes more disrupted with aging, making older adults more vulnerable to schedule changes (including meal timing).
- "Circadian dead zone": a period where light exposure has little/no shifting effect on the clock.

## Pre-trip and travel protocols
- For a 9-hour eastward shift (e.g., California to Europe): 2-3 days before travel, shift wake time and light exposure (via artificial light, since sun isn't up yet), and optionally exercise/eat at the new target time, to pre-advance the clock.
- Simply "getting sunlight when you land" often fails for large eastward shifts — you're likely to hit either the circadian dead zone or the pre-temperature-minimum window, which delays (shifts you the wrong direction).
- With adequate prep (a day or so before departure plus continuing on arrival), a full circadian shift can potentially be accomplished within 24-36 hours.
- Eat according to the local meal schedule for alertness once traveling (fasting is fine if that's your practice, but time eating windows to local schedule).
- For westward travel (the "easier" direction): use caffeine, exercise, and light/sunlight in the evening (after temperature peak, e.g., around 6-8 PM) to stay awake longer and delay the clock as needed.
- Avoid taking naps intended to be short (20 min-1hr) upon arrival — risk of oversleeping 4+ hours and then waking at midnight unable to fall back asleep.
- Short-trip rule: if a trip is 48 hours or less, stay on your home schedule; by 72 hours you start running into real jet-lag trouble. Rule of thumb: 3 days or less, stay on home schedule as much as possible.

## Melatonin
- Melatonin is released from the pineal gland and induces sleepiness; it helps you FALL asleep but does not help you STAY asleep.
- Huberman's stance: melatonin is not his first recommendation as a circadian-shifting tool because of its endocrine side effects; he prefers behavioral tools (light, temperature, exercise) which are more titratable and have larger safety margins.
- Mechanistic chain: melatonin inhibits GnRH (gonadotropin-releasing hormone) release from the hypothalamus → GnRH normally stimulates luteinizing hormone (LH) → LH stimulates estrogen release from ovaries (females) and testosterone release from Sertoli cells of the testes (males, note: actually Leydig cells produce testosterone — as stated in transcript, Sertoli cells mentioned). So melatonin is inhibitory to GnRH → LH → sex hormone release.
- If used, should ideally be done under guidance of a doctor/circadian specialist; use in the afternoon/evening only to promote falling asleep, not as a default circadian-realignment tool for most people.

## Shift work protocol
- Rule of thumb: stay on the same shift schedule for at least 14 days, including weekends, if at all possible.
- Even non-shift-workers should avoid large weekend schedule shifts (e.g., "sleeping in" on Sunday) since consistency matters broadly.
- During a shift (example: 4 PM-2 AM), view as much light as safely possible during the work/wake period; avoid light and seek darkness during the intended sleep period.
- Simplified heuristic: if body temperature is decreasing, avoid light; if body temperature is increasing, seek light.

## Sleep-deprivation / caregiving / infant sleep
- Babies are not born with a typical sleep-wake cycle; melatonin in infants is released at a fairly constant (non-cyclic/phasic) level rather than a 24-hour cycle, and is present in high concentration per unit volume due to small body size, decreasing proportionally as the baby grows.
- For parents or caregivers unable to sleep continuously (infant care, caring for a sick loved one): the key goal is keeping the autonomic nervous system from tipping into heightened alertness states during would-be sleep periods.
- Non-Sleep Deep Rest (NSDR) protocols are recommended for this — Huberman describes personally waking at 3 AM, doing an NSDR protocol, and falling back asleep, then waking at 6:30 AM feeling rested.
- NSDR is framed as a way to "teach your brain/nervous system how to turn off thoughts" via using the body to shift the mind (rather than trying to force thoughts off directly) — described as free, research-backed, and usable without medication except when truly needed.
- Recommendation for caregivers/shift-disrupted people: get sleep whenever possible, but also try to get morning and evening sunlight exposure (or artificial light if unavailable).

## Key summary heuristics stated
- Know your temperature minimum.
- Light in the early part of the day is valuable for alertness/clock-setting; sunlight is preferable to screens but both work.
- Avoid light in the 4-6 hours before your temperature minimum (unless deliberately trying to delay the clock, e.g., westward travel).
- Increasing body temperature (e.g., cold exposure/ice bath aftereffect, or post-hot-shower rebound) can help advance/shift the clock; decreasing temperature (e.g., hot shower's cooling aftereffect) can help delay it.
