# The Most Effective Weight Training, Cardio & Nutrition for Women | Dr. Lauren Colenso-Semple
- Guest: Dr. Lauren Colenso-Semple (PhD, integrative physiology; certified strength & conditioning specialist)
- URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Q4XT82yd-Q

## Sex differences in muscle/training response
- Muscle protein synthesis response to exercise/nutrition is essentially identical between men and women; no meaningful sex difference in growth signaling.
- Baseline muscularity differs because puberty testosterone surge in males raises baseline muscle mass, but once training starts, men and women gain similar *relative* size.
- Reference range for male testosterone roughly 300-900 ng/dL (up to ~1,200 in some countries); within the normal range, higher testosterone does not predict greater muscle response to training — same holds for women.
- Female bodybuilders using supraphysiological (exogenous) androgens can approach male-level muscularity, but this doesn't generalize to natural training advice — normal-range hormone variation doesn't predict outcomes.
- Post-exercise acute rises in testosterone/growth hormone are not the driver of long-term hypertrophy (old theory now considered outdated); chasing that acute hormonal spike (e.g., via high-rep/superset schemes) doesn't improve long-term gains.
- No sex-specific programming, rep range, or nutrient-timing is supported by data — "men and women respond to exercise very similarly."

## Resistance training structure & progression
- For a full-body program: target all major muscle groups, use a load that is a "sufficient stimulus" — training close enough to failure (able to do ~10 but not 11 = failure; stopping at 8-9 of a possible 10 is appropriate).
- Training 2-3x/week: do full-body sessions each time. Training 4 days/week: upper/lower split. Training 5-6 days/week: more granular splits (e.g., push/pull/legs).
- Work sets per muscle group per session: at least 2, guest prefers 3, up to 4 is fine; beyond 4 sets per exercise likely unnecessary. At 3x/week full-body with 3-4 sets/session, quads end up getting ~9-12 sets/week.
- Rest intervals: ~2 minutes for most exercises/machines/dumbbells; ~3 minutes (or 4-5 min) for heavy compound lifts like squat/deadlift when training for strength/1RM specifically. Recommends autoregulating rest rather than rigid timing.
- Agonist-antagonist supersets (e.g., bench press paired with rows) are time-efficient and don't appear to interfere with adaptation or acute performance compared to straight sets with full rest.
- Forced reps: little added benefit beyond fun with a training partner. Drop sets: good "finisher," useful variety especially for cable/isolation exercises (bicep curl, leg extension) — not superior to straight sets but a legitimate time-efficient tool.
- Movement speed: move the weight as fast as possible during the hard (concentric) phase, control the easier phase; don't intentionally slow reps down — the natural slowing near failure is not something to force.
- Partial reps/isometric holds/tempo variation are discouraged for hypertrophy progression tracking — they add variables that make it hard to know if you're truly progressing (recommends standardizing ROM, exercise, and tempo, and progressing via load or reps only).
- Rep ranges: max-strength (1RM) training requires low-rep, high-load work; for hypertrophy, wide flexibility — similar growth from low-rep/high-load through high-rep/low-load, provided training is close to failure, as long as total volume is matched. Guest's personal preference: 6-12 reps typically; recommends beginners use 8-12 to build movement proficiency before exploring lower/higher rep ranges.
- Varying rep ranges: can combine different rep-range days within the same week per lift (e.g., a heavier-day and a moderate-rep day for squats) rather than block-periodizing by 3-week phases — preserves a consistent "anchor point" for tracking progress.
- High-rep sets (15-25 reps) on compound movements are, in the guest's clinical experience, injured people "at least as often" as heavy low-rep sets — attributed to difficulty maintaining perfect form/concentration under high-rep fatigue.
- Two 20-minute full-body resistance workouts per week is described as sufficient to meaningfully offset age-related muscle loss and reduce fall/fracture risk.
- No dedicated mobility work needed if training through full range of motion with a proper warm-up (light warm-up sets through full ROM serve this purpose).
- Ab work: not a fat-loss method — hypertrophying rectus abdominis requires progressive loading like any muscle; spot reduction via crunches doesn't reduce belly fat.

## Menstrual cycle, hormonal contraception, menopause
- No need to change training based on menstrual cycle phase; recommendation is to train hard, consistently, and progressively regardless of phase, adjusting only if symptoms (cramps, fatigue, low motivation) warrant skipping/adjusting a workout.
- Survey data: 75-80% of women report menstrual symptoms (cramps, low back pain, irritability), but the large majority don't change training in response; most symptomatic days number only 1-2 per cycle.
- Subjective performance (feeling like a lift is harder) often diverges from objective performance (same weight actually lifted) — applies to menstrual symptoms, sleep deprivation, jet lag, stress alike.
- Combined oral contraceptive pills: data shows no measurable effect (positive or negative) on strength, hypertrophy, or power outcomes — consistent with the fact that even endogenous hormone fluctuations across the natural cycle don't move these outcomes.
- No evidence that menopause/perimenopause hormonal shifts directly accelerate muscle loss; age-related muscle loss is attributed primarily to physical inactivity, not declining estrogen per se.
- Nutrition-across-cycle-phase claims (eat differently by phase) rest on poor, mostly self-reported observational data; no data support changing caloric, protein, or carb intake by cycle phase.
- Menopausal hormone therapy (HRT): effective for symptom management (hot flashes, sleep, night sweats) but current data do NOT support claims that it prevents cognitive decline or cardiovascular disease, or preserves/increases muscle mass — those benefits are not established.
- References the early-2000s Women's Health Initiative trial: one arm was stopped early due to signal of increased (not decreased) cardiovascular risk; media over-generalized this into "hormone therapy causes heart disease/cancer," which misrepresented a complex, age/status-dependent dataset.
- Testosterone therapy for women is only well-supported for treating low libido/sexual desire; effective doses for this use are low ("low normal") — going beyond that risks masculinizing side effects (voice deepening, hair growth) without evidence of muscle benefit.
- Body-fat redistribution to the midsection during menopause transition is a common complaint; mechanism (e.g., estrogen receptor changes) is hypothesized but not confirmed by human data (would require fat biopsy, which is more uncomfortable than muscle biopsy).

## Cortisol myths
- Cortisol should be high in the morning (drives waking) and low in the hours before sleep — true for both sexes.
- "Cortisol causes belly fat" narrative (moon face, midsection fat storage) is a real phenomenon only in pathological Cushing's syndrome (chronic pathologically high cortisol, usually from steroid use) — not applicable to normal, transient exercise/stress-induced cortisol elevations.
- A single resistance training session of ~30-60 minutes can triple or quadruple circulating cortisol acutely — this is normal and necessary, not harmful, and is not equivalent to Cushing's-level chronic elevation.
- Acute cortisol fluctuations are necessary for blood pressure regulation and blood glucose regulation, and do not by themselves cause fat storage or block fat loss.
- Apparent "cortisol makes you fat" correlations in real life are usually confounded by comfort eating during stress (a "cryptic correlation" — stress raises cortisol AND drives some people to overeat, but the food intake, not the cortisol, is the operative factor).

## Training timing / session length / individual factors
- Guest personally trains fasted if training in the morning; eats first if training later in day; always takes caffeine pre-workout.
- ~90% of adults worldwide consume caffeine daily, which makes running clean caffeine-naive research studies difficult (2-week washout needed, confounded by withdrawal effects).
- Fed vs. fasted resistance training: long-term studies show similar muscle growth and fat loss adaptations in men and women regardless of fasted state; the early-2010s "fasted cardio maximizes fat loss" idea (based on acute fat oxidation) doesn't hold up because acute post-workout fat oxidation isn't large enough to change long-term body fat.
- Pre-workout food doesn't function as direct fuel for a typical (non-glycogen-depleting) resistance session, since eaten food must still be digested/absorbed; choice of fed/fasted before lifting should be based on personal preference/comfort, not performance necessity.
- The old "anabolic window" (must eat protein within ~30-60 min post-workout or gains are lost) is outdated — elevated muscle protein synthesis after resistance training remains elevated up to 24 hours later; total daily/weekly protein intake matters far more than per-meal timing.
- A study (guest recalls, ~1.5 years prior) suggested people can utilize up to 100 g of protein per feeding for muscle protein synthesis, overturning the older "only ~30 g protein per meal is usable" assumption.
- Guest's own training after 14 years: currently trains more frequently (upper push / upper pull / lower body rotation) for ~30-45 minutes per session rather than fewer, longer sessions, to sustain habit consistency; typically trains close to failure (occasionally to failure, especially on machines where it's low-risk).
- Switching workout time-of-day (e.g., evening to morning) can cause a temporary performance dip that resolves within a couple of weeks.
- Training in the evening can disrupt sleep for some people; training earlier in the day (before ~10am) is linked (anecdotally, by both guest and host) to more energy for the rest of the day versus midday training.

## Creatine
- Recommendation for women is identical to men: creatine monohydrate, standard dose ~5 g/day.
- Gummy creatine supplements are flagged as unreliable — an analysis of top-selling Amazon creatine gummies found some contained virtually no creatine; powder form recommended over gummies.
- Effect size described as modest but real: "an extra rep or two" or "cut a second off your sprint" — well-studied and considered very safe long-term (including at 5 g/day for years).
- No benefit to taking creatine without a concurrent training stimulus — won't increase muscle mass on its own.
- Creatine's cognitive/brain-health benefit claims are called premature — supporting data come from deficit populations only (Alzheimer's patients, clinically depressed patients on SSRIs, severe sleep deprivation, traumatic brain injury), not healthy people.
- Creatine can raise blood creatinine levels (may flag as "high" on a standard blood panel) — this is a benign, expected artifact of creatine metabolism, not kidney damage; tell your physician you're supplementing.
- No evidence that alternative creatine forms (vs. monohydrate) reduce subcutaneous water retention/bloating claims marketed to women — creatine monohydrate remains best-studied; dry-scooping vs. dissolving fully makes no absorption difference.

## Cardio / conditioning
- No specific added cardio needed if lifting 2-3x/week and otherwise physically active (sports, hiking, etc.) — unless there's a specific endurance/sprint performance goal.
- Concurrent training "interference effect" (endurance training blunting hypertrophy) is real only under high training volumes stacked very close together; for most people, doing resistance training first and separating cardio by several hours (or different days) avoids meaningful interference.
- High-intensity cardio's main advantage over longer moderate-intensity cardio is time efficiency — physiological adaptations can be similar for equivalent overall training stimulus.
- Cardio (fasted or fed) doesn't require caffeine "need" but guest personally prefers caffeine for most training.
- On step counts: no strong evidence for a specific magic number (references the commonly cited but unsubstantiated ~7,000-10,000 steps/day); step-count focus is most valuable for previously-sedentary people going from very low activity to moderate; less useful as a target for people already broadly active.
- Adding cardio purely for weight/fat loss is characterized as inefficient — fat loss from added exercise alone is "pretty disappointing" relative to fat loss achievable via dietary/caloric adjustment.

## Other protocols/claims mentioned
- Deliberate cold exposure: benefits are more likely psychological/mental-clarity-related than physiological, per guest ("depends").
- Zone 2 training: fine and necessary for long-term endurance goals, but not obligatory if you prefer higher-intensity or other activity.
- Weighted vest walking: explicitly said to NOT provide adequate stimulus for muscle or bone — not a substitute for resistance training (vest use during loaded squats/lunges/jumps could be useful, walking alone is not).
- Grip strength/arm-hang test as longevity proxy: guest says grip strength does correlate with longevity/overall strength in clinical literature, but recommends focusing on full-body resistance training rather than training grip specifically.
- Recommends unilateral/balance-incorporating exercises (e.g., walking lunges, single-leg RDL) within resistance training rather than isolated balance training, for fall-prevention/longevity purposes.
- Ice baths and NSAIDs: both can blunt/interfere with the hypertrophic adaptation because they reduce the inflammatory signaling needed for muscle growth adaptation — trade-off between faster subjective recovery and full adaptation.
- Guest's favorite lesser-used exercise: single-leg Romanian deadlift (RDL) for glute/hamstring work with an optional balance component.
- Overtraining at individual-workout level: guest considers it rare for typical (especially female) recreational trainees; true overtraining shows up as sleep disruption, prolonged soreness, feeling run down — most people are not at meaningful overtraining risk.
- Guest's professional output: co-writes a monthly research review ("Mass Research Review") with Eric Trexler, Eric Helms, and Michael Zourdos covering health/fitness/wellness science translation.
